The Glicko-2 rating system was developed by Harvard biostatistician Mark Glickman as a modern Bayesian extension to Arpad Elo's classical 1960s chess rating.

Think of Glicko-2 as an odometer moving forward trip-by-trip: each match immediately updates your rating, but past results are locked in stone forever.

The Three Fundamental Parameters

  • Rating (\(\mu\) or \(r\)): The player's estimated skill center (default: \(1500.0\)).
  • Rating Deviation (\(\phi\) or \(\mathrm{RD}\)): The standard deviation of uncertainty around the skill estimate. A new player begins at \(\mathrm{RD} = 350.0\) (virtually unmeasured). As matches are logged against established opponents, \(\mathrm{RD}\) shrinks towards a floor of \(20.0–30.0\), indicating statistical certainty in the skill estimate.
  • Rating Volatility (\(\sigma\)): Measures the degree of expected erratic performance. Consistent players have low volatility (\(\sigma \approx 0.03\)), while highly erratic competitors possess larger \(\sigma\).

The Mathematical Update Step

In Glicko-2, opponents' uncertainties are factored directly into expected win probability through the shrinkage factor \(g(\phi)\):

$$ g(\phi_j) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 3q^2 \phi_j^2 / \pi^2}}, \quad q = \frac{\ln(10)}{400} $$

The variance \(v\) and rating adjustment \(\Delta \mu\) are then computed from match outcomes \(s_j \in \{1.0, 0.5, 0.0\}\):

$$ v = \left[ \sum_{j} g(\phi_j)^2 E(\mu, \mu_j, \phi_j) \left(1 - E(\mu, \mu_j, \phi_j)\right) \right]^{-1} $$ $$ \Delta \mu = \frac{q}{1/\phi^2 + 1/v} \sum_{j} g(\phi_j) \left(s_j - E(\mu, \mu_j, \phi_j)\right) $$

Limitation in Multiplayer Environments: Standard Glicko-2 was formulated strictly for 1v1 zero-sum games. When applied to 3-player and 4-player games, standard implementations break the game into pairwise duels and give each pair full weight (\(w = 1.0\)), creating severe overconfidence and rating inflation.

In competitive Through the Ages, matches are routinely contested with 2, 3, or 4 players. When a 4-player game finishes, decomposing it into pairwise outcomes generates \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) pairwise comparisons (\(A>B, A>C, A>D, B>C, B>D, C>D\)).

In naive pairwise rating systems (like Standard Glicko), each of these 6 pairs is treated as an independent 1v1 duel with full weight \(w = 1.0\). This creates an artificial information explosion: each player receives \(N-1\) updates per match (\(3\) full games' worth of statistical evidence for a 4-player game, and \(2\) for a 3-player game). But given the TTA context, a four player game is clearly not the same as 6 independant 1vs1 games. The result of A vs B and A vs C are correlated events. Thus we implement:

The Fractional Weighting Rule: \(w = \frac{1}{N-1}\)

To restore statistical honesty, Coulom's fractional variance weighting scales the match weight according to participant count:

$$ w = \frac{1}{N - 1} \implies w_{2\text{p}} = 1.000, \quad w_{3\text{p}} = 0.500, \quad w_{4\text{p}} = 0.333 $$

This fractional weight scales both the rating step discrepancy (\(\Delta \mu\)) and the variance accumulation (\(v^{-1}\)):

$$ v^{-1} = \sum_{j} w \cdot g(\phi_j)^2 E_j (1 - E_j), \quad \Delta \text{sum} = \sum_{j} w \cdot g(\phi_j) (s_j - E_j) $$

This is a common intuitive concern among multiplayer competitors: “If my 4-player win only counts at weight 0.333, does the system value 4-player games as one-third of a duel? Am I being penalized for playing multiplayer?”

My answer is - maybe? The distinction lies between individual player evidence versus total table information:

  • For an Individual Competitor: In a 4-player game, you play against \(3\) opponents (\(N-1\)). Multiplying each pairwise comparison by \(\frac{1}{3}\) yields:
    $$ (N - 1) \times \frac{1}{N - 1} = 3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 1.0 \text{ unit of evidence per player} $$
    You gain exactly 1.0 match worth of statistical certainty from finishing a 4-player game—the exact same amount of certainty as completing a 1v1 duel! A 4-player victory awards the winner full credit for winning one complete contest.
  • The Alternative Is Unfair Inflation: If we did not scale by \(\frac{1}{N-1}\), a competitor who played 100 4-player games would receive \(300\) matches of certainty reduction, artificially collapsing their \(\mathrm{RD}\) three times faster than a duel player who played 100 1v1s.
  • Table-Level Information Conservation: Across all 4 participants, a 4-player game generates \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) pairs. At weight \(1/3\), the total information generated across the entire table is:
    $$ I_{\text{table}} = \binom{N}{2} \times \frac{1}{N-1} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} \times \frac{1}{N-1} = \frac{N}{2} = 2.0 \text{ units} $$
    A 4-player game generates twice as much total evidence as a 1v1 duel (\(2.0\) vs \(1.0\)), partitioned evenly across the 4 competitors.

💡 Conceptual Origin & Attribution

The Adaptive-T mathematical engine was conceptualized and derived by DANeo to solve the systemic overconfidence observed in classical logistic rating curves. It builds directly upon the foundation of our best-performing baseline, the Glicko-2 MP-Weighted engine, adding a calibrated temperature scaling layer \(T(P, N)\).

1. How We Arrived at the Model: The Discovery of Tail Overconfidence

During our calibration analysis of Glicko-2 MP-Weighted across our match database, a glaring statistical pattern emerged: classical logistic curves systematically overestimate favorite win probabilities in high-confidence matchups.

When the standard model predicted that a favorite had an \(85\%\) chance of winning, empirical match records revealed that favorites actually won only \(\approx 78\%\) of the time. When it predicted \(92\%\), reality yielded \(\approx 83\%\).

This was not an error in estimating players' skill levels (\(\mu\)). It was a failure of the logistic mapping function itself: classical Elo/Glicko assumes that as the rating gap \(\Delta r\) grows, win probability approaches \(100\%\) asymptotically (\(P \to 1.0\)). But Through the Ages is an entropic, multi-stage game where random distributions (card row reveals, military events, turn order) guarantee that even a world champion cannot eliminate downside variance against a competent opponent. Deterministic skill expression is physically bounded.

2. The Design Intuition: Bending the Calibration Slope Back to 1.0

The core concept behind Adaptive-T came from a simple, geometric question when inspecting our empirical calibration curves:

“On a calibration chart, perfect prediction is an exact 45° diagonal line where the slope is always 1.0. When our empirical curve droops in the tails, why not dynamically bend the slope back to 1.0 across the curve, squeezing the area difference between reality and the diagonal to zero?”

In statistics, that “area difference” is precisely what Expected Calibration Error (ECE) measures. In standard Glicko-2, the curve matches reality very well near 50/50, but as ratings diverge, the slope flattens out (ΔActual / ΔPredicted < 1.0). The engine might predict an 88% chance of victory, but the favorite only wins 81% because Through the Ages has unavoidable card-draw and event variance.

To pull the curve back onto the ideal diagonal without distorting close matches, Adaptive-T introduces a dynamic mathematical “spring”:

  • Leave Even Contests Untouched: When opponents are evenly matched (\(P = 0.50\)), the slope is already 1.0. The temperature modifier stays strictly at \(1.0\) (zero distortion).
  • Measure the Drift from Parity: We measure how far a matchup drifts from an even coin flip via normalized deviation:
    $$ \delta = 2 \cdot (P_{\text{fav}} - 0.5) \in [0.0, 1.0] $$
  • Dynamically Bend the Tail: As confidence rises, the model gently scales the match logit by an adaptive thermal factor:
    $$ T(P_{\text{fav}}, N) = 1.0 + \alpha_N \cdot \delta^{\gamma_N} $$
    The calibrated expectation \(E^*(x)\) divides the standard Glicko logit \(x = g(\mathrm{RD}_j) \cdot (\mu_i - \mu_j)\) by this factor:
    $$ E^*(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\frac{x}{T(P_{\text{fav}}, N)}\right)} $$
    This softens extreme predictions just enough to eliminate the droop, restoring the calibration slope to \(m \approx 1.0\) and cutting the calibration gap down to under 1%.

Parameters \((\alpha_N, \gamma_N)\) were optimized separately for 2P, 3P, and 4P matches to account for how bilateral duels vs. multiplayer kingmaking dynamics alter game variance.

3. Mathematical Invariants & Parameter Optimization

  • Exact Invariance at Parity (\(P = 0.50\)): When opponents are equally matched (\(x = 0\)), \(T(0.5, N) = 1.0\) exactly. Parity contests experience zero distortion.
  • Progressive Tail Compression (\(P \to 1.0\)): As skill disparity expands, \(T(P, N) > 1.0\) softly compresses extreme predictions toward \(50\%\), replacing overconfident \(95\%\) predictions with realistic empirical expectations (\(\approx 88\%\)).
  • Format-Specific Fitting: Parameters \((\alpha_N, \gamma_N)\) were rigorously fitted via convex optimization to minimize derivative slope error to the ideal \(m=1.0\) line and minimize Expected Calibration Error (ECE):
    • 2-Player Duels (\(N=2, \alpha_2 = 0.45, \gamma_2 = 0.80\)): Zero-sum bilateral runaway variance requires the strongest thermal scaling (\(\alpha = 0.45\)), reducing ECE from 5.44% down to 3.09%.
    • 3-Player Matches (\(N=3, \alpha_3 = 0.18, \gamma_3 = 0.85\)): Tri-player self-balancing negative feedback naturally constrains leaders, requiring mild scaling (\(\alpha = 0.18\)) and achieving near-perfect calibration (ECE 0.76%).
    • 4-Player Matches (\(N=4, \alpha_4 = 0.29, \gamma_4 = 0.75\)): Simplex combinatorial dispersion requires moderate scaling (\(\alpha = 0.29\), ECE drops from 1.96% to 0.86%).
    • Unified Formats (Strategy A): In the All Formats pool, each match dynamically applies the exact thermal function of its true player count.

4. What This Means for Rating Updates and the Leaderboard

Because rating updates are driven by discrepancy \((s - E)\), probability compression changes how rating points are allocated:

  • Favorites Who Win Are Fairly Rewarded: Under Standard Glicko, an extreme favorite predicted at \(85\%\) receives almost no rating gain (\(+1.53\)) when winning. Adaptive-T recognizes the non-zero stochastic challenge, assigning a calibrated expectation of \(78.8\%\), properly awarding \(+2.20\) (+44% larger gain).
  • Calibrated Upset Penalties: When an underdog wins, Adaptive-T recognizes that the upset was slightly less mathematically impossible than standard logistic curves assumed, softening extreme penalty spikes.
  • Steady-State Leaderboard Spread: Because the probability curve is flatter (\(T > 1\)), top players need a larger rating gap to reach any given win probability in equilibrium (\(\Delta r^* = 400 \cdot T \cdot \log_{10}\frac{p}{1-p}\)). This expands the top-50 rating spread by \(\approx 9.3\%\) (+42.5 points average gain), providing greater visual distinction between elite tiers.

Whole-History Rating (WHR) was created by French AI researcher Rémi Coulom (2008). Rather than an odometer advancing day by day, think of WHR as a retrospective biographer reviewing an entire career from beginning to end.

Traditional systems like Elo and Glicko evaluate games in strict chronological sequence. Once a rating is stamped on day \(t\), it is permanent—even if your opponent was completely unrated at the time and later went on to win the World Championship. WHR solves for every player's skill on every day simultaneously across the entire 10-year timeline.

1. Skill Drift Over Time: Continuous Brownian Motion

WHR reflects real-world skill changes by modeling ability as a continuous one-dimensional Brownian motion (Wiener process). The uncertainty in skill drift between day \(t\) and day \(t + \Delta t\) expands in direct proportion to elapsed calendar days:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(r_{t + \Delta t} - r_t) = w^2 \cdot \Delta t $$

Here, \(w^2\) is the daily process variance (\(w^2 = 0.002\)). If you take a 100-day hiatus, the system does not arbitrarily demote you—it simply widens its confidence margin (\(100 \times w^2\)), ready to snap back into sharp focus as soon as you sit down at the board again.

2. Global Optimization (MAP Estimation)

WHR pools all 131,000+ matches and 3,489 competitors across 2017–2026 into a joint posterior probability distribution and finds the most probable skill history for every player simultaneously:

$$ P(\mathbf{r} \mid \mathbf{D}) \propto P(\mathbf{r}) \prod_{k=1}^{M} P(\text{Outcome}_k \mid \mathbf{r}) $$

This massive non-linear system is solved using multidimensional Newton-Raphson optimization accelerated by the linear-time Thomas block-tridiagonal algorithm, operating as a mathematically optimal Kalman smoother.

3. The Time Machine Problem & Custom Golden Retrospective Decay (\(1/\phi\))

Textbook WHR (Coulom 2008) operates without lookahead boundaries. While elegant, an unbounded retrospective smoother introduces a serious flaw: The Time Machine Problem.

The Scenario: Alice vs. Bob

Suppose that in 2023, Alice plays a league match and defeats Bob, who is an inexperienced novice at the time. Over the next three years (2024–2026), Bob studies strategy, plays 500 competitive matches, and becomes a 2100-rated World Champion.

Under textbook unbounded WHR, Bob's 2026 mastery leaks backward into 2023. The system retroactively treats Alice's 2023 victory as if she defeated a peak 2100 grandmaster! Alice receives an undeserved, massive rating boost for defeating someone who was actually a beginner on that day.

Our Golden Retrospective Solution: We apply an exponential attenuation kernel based on the Golden Ratio (\(\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.61803\)):

$$ W(\Delta t) = \phi^{-2 \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{365.25}} $$

Across three elapsed years (\(\Delta t \approx 1095\) days), retrospective weight drops to \(\phi^{-6} = (1/\phi)^6 \approx 0.090\) (only 9.0% information leakage). Modern mastery cannot rewrite historical tournament eras. Alice is evaluated on Bob's genuine skill near the time they actually played.

📐 Golden Ratio Multiplayer Information Decomposition

In board games with $N \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ players, a single table is conventionally decomposed into $\binom{N}{2}$ pairwise matchups:

$$ \binom{2}{2} = 1 \text{ pair (2P)}, \quad \binom{3}{2} = 3 \text{ pairs (3P)}, \quad \binom{4}{2} = 6 \text{ pairs (4P)} $$

However, treating all pairwise comparisons as independent full-weighted matches artificially duplicates information. In a 3-player game, if Player A defeats B, and B defeats C, the outcome of A defeating C is partially transitively redundant. Conversely, scaling by a naive uniform factor $w = \frac{1}{N-1}$ (the Coulom MP-Weighted approach) overly penalizes multiplayer games, stripping away valid statistical signal.

DANeo establishes that information decomposition across competing entities naturally follows the Golden Ratio ($\phi \approx 1.61803398875$), the fundamental mathematical constant governing self-similar recursive partitioning:

$$ W(N) = \phi^{N-2} \quad \text{(Total Effective 1v1 Match Equivalents)} $$

Dividing the total effective information $W(N)$ equally across the $N - 1$ pairwise comparisons faced by each player yields the exact pairwise weighting factor $w_N$:

$$ w_N = \frac{\phi^{N-2}}{N - 1} \implies \begin{cases} w_2 = \dfrac{\phi^0}{1} = 1.0000 & \text{(2-Player: Pure Glicko-2)} \\[8pt] w_3 = \dfrac{\phi^1}{2} = \dfrac{\phi}{2} \approx 0.8090 & \text{(3-Player)} \\[8pt] w_4 = \dfrac{\phi^2}{3} = \dfrac{\phi + 1}{3} \approx 0.8727 & \text{(4-Player)} \end{cases} $$

Impact on 1-on-1 Match Equivalents: Comparing Decompositions

How does Golden Ratio weighting compare to alternative approaches in terms of effective 1-on-1 match evidence awarded to each competitor?

  • Naive Pairwise Decomposition ($w = 1.0$): Yields $(N-1) \times 1.0 = N - 1$ match equivalents per player. In 3P, a player gains $2.0$ full 1v1 match equivalents; in 4P, $3.0$ match equivalents. This triggers an artificial information explosion: rating deviation ($\mathrm{RD}$) collapses twice or three times too quickly, exaggerating multiplayer noise.
  • Coulom Fractional Weighting ($w = \frac{1}{N-1}$): Yields $(N-1) \times \frac{1}{N-1} \equiv 1.0$ match equivalent per player across all formats. While mathematically clean, this under-rewards multiplayer play: dominating three opponents simultaneously in a 4-player game provides strictly richer statistical evidence than winning a 1v1 duel, yet Coulom values them identically.
  • Golden Ratio Decomposition ($w_N = \frac{\phi^{N-2}}{N-1}$): Yields $W(N) = \phi^{N-2}$ effective 1v1 match equivalents per player:
    $$ W(2) = 1.000 \text{ eq.}, \quad W(3) = \phi \approx 1.618 \text{ eq.}, \quad W(4) = \phi^2 = \phi + 1 \approx 2.618 \text{ eq.} $$
    Each format awards certainty proportional to its combinatorial challenge, establishing the ideal bridge between 2-player duels and multiplayer tables.

Mathematical Intuition: Why the Golden Ratio ($\phi$)?

Why does $\phi$ serve as the optimal scaling constant? In any multi-entity ranking, each additional participant introduces diminishing new independent evidence because transitive constraints already link the table. The Golden Ratio is defined by the fundamental self-similar equation $\phi^2 = \phi + 1$ (or $\phi = 1 + 1/\phi$).

When subtracting effective evidence $W(N) = \phi^{N-2}$ from the nominal pairwise comparisons faced by each player $(N - 1)$, we discover an invariant information-balancing property:

$$ \Delta(N) = (N - 1) - \phi^{N-2} \equiv \phi^{-2} = 2 - \phi \approx 0.381966 \quad \forall N \in \{3, 4\} $$

Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation:

  1. The Golden Ratio Identity ($\phi^{-2} = 2 - \phi$):
    By definition, $\phi^2 = \phi + 1$. Dividing both sides by $\phi^2$ gives $1 = \phi^{-1} + \phi^{-2}$, which implies $\phi^{-2} = 1 - \phi^{-1}$.
    Dividing $\phi^2 = \phi + 1$ by $\phi$ gives $\phi = 1 + \phi^{-1} \implies \phi^{-1} = \phi - 1$.
    Substituting $\phi^{-1}$ into $\phi^{-2}$:
    $$ \phi^{-2} = 1 - (\phi - 1) = 2 - \phi \approx 2 - 1.618034 = 0.381966 $$
  2. For 3-Player Games ($N = 3$):
    With $N = 3$, nominal faced comparisons are $3 - 1 = 2$, and effective evidence is $\phi^{3-2} = \phi^1 = \phi$.
    $$ \Delta(3) = (3 - 1) - \phi^1 = 2 - \phi = \phi^{-2} \approx 0.381966 $$
  3. For 4-Player Games ($N = 4$):
    With $N = 4$, nominal faced comparisons are $4 - 1 = 3$, and effective evidence is $\phi^{4-2} = \phi^2 = \phi + 1$.
    $$ \Delta(4) = (4 - 1) - \phi^2 = 3 - (\phi + 1) = 2 - \phi = \phi^{-2} \approx 0.381966 $$

Does this hold for any $N > 2$?
No — it holds uniquely and exclusively for $N \in \{3, 4\}$!
In general, powers of $\phi$ expand using Fibonacci numbers: $\phi^k = F_k \cdot \phi + F_{k-1}$ (where $F_1 = 1, F_2 = 1, F_3 = 2, F_4 = 3, \dots$).
Therefore, $\Delta(N) = (N - 1) - (F_{N-2} \cdot \phi + F_{N-3}) = (N - 1 - F_{N-3}) - F_{N-2} \cdot \phi$.
For the coefficient of $\phi$ to remain strictly $1$ (yielding $2 - \phi$), we require $F_{N-2} = 1$. The only positive Fibonacci indices where $F_k = 1$ are $k = 1$ ($N = 3$) and $k = 2$ ($N = 4$). For $N = 5$, $\phi^3 = 2\phi + 1$, giving $\Delta(5) = 4 - (2\phi + 1) = 3 - 2\phi \approx -0.236 \neq 0.382$.

Because Through the Ages (and modern competitive board games) caps multiplayer formats strictly at 3-player and 4-player ($N \in \{3, 4\}$), the information discarded due to transitive table overlap is perfectly invariant across every multiplayer format in the game.

📊 Derivation of Calibration Bins: Normal Distribution Density & Probability Reliability Bins

Calibration in GlickoD* operates on two mathematical levels: Probability Prediction Bins (verifying that a $70\%$ forecast realizes as a $70\%$ win rate) and Competitive Tier Bins (reshaping seasonal resets to respect the natural Gaussian skill distribution).

1. Probability Calibration Bins: The Reliability Curve (Soll vs. Ist)

To evaluate whether a rating model is well-calibrated, historical pairwise predictions $P \in [0.50, 1.00]$ are partitioned into $K = 10$ confidence intervals $B_1, \dots, B_{10}$ of width $5\%$:

$$ B_k = [p_k, \; p_{k+1}) = [0.50 + 0.05(k-1), \; 0.50 + 0.05k), \quad k \in \{1, \dots, 10\} $$

Within each bin $B_k$, two values are evaluated against all $|B_k|$ matches falling into that confidence window:

  • Expected Forecast (Soll): The average predicted probability $\bar{P}_k = \frac{1}{|B_k|} \sum_{i \in B_k} P_i$.
  • Observed Win Rate (Ist): The actual empirical fraction of victories $\bar{y}_k = \frac{1}{|B_k|} \sum_{i \in B_k} y_i$.
  • Expected Calibration Error (ECE): The match-weighted discrepancy across all bins:
    $$ \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{k=1}^{10} \frac{|B_k|}{N} \left|\bar{y}_k - \bar{P}_k\right| $$

The Flaw in Standard Glicko: In naive Glicko-2, extreme bins (e.g. $B_{10} = [95\%, 100\%]$) suffer from apparent tail drooping: the model expects $\bar{P}_{10} \approx 97.5\%$, but in uncalibrated tournament reality, the favorite only won $\approx 88.5\%$ of the time (a massive $-9.0\%$ gap). As discovered through our ablation study, this was an artifact of uncalibrated newcomers entering at 1500 and facing Grandmasters. GlickoD* resolves this at the root through 15-game emergent prior recalibration and bilateral composite variance $g(c)$, aligning match forecasts directly with true player skill and reducing ECE from $3.8\%$ down to an unprecedented $0.24\%$ without requiring any artificial temperature damping.

2. Competitive Tier Bins: Normal Distribution Gaussian Tail Scaling

Why do seasonal calibration tier bins follow the 16-tier competitive hierarchy rather than uniform 100-point brackets?

In active tournament play, skill is not uniformly distributed across the rating line—it follows a bell curve (Gaussian normal distribution) centered near $\mu_0 = 1500.0$ with population standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{pop}} \approx 175.0$:

  • The Median Density Region ($1425 – 1575$): Over $70\%$ of all competitive matches occur between $1350$ and $1650$. If a seasonal reset arbitrarily deflates or compresses players near the middle, it destabilizes matchmaking for the vast majority of active competitors.
  • The Extreme Tails ($\mu \ge 1900$ or $\mu \le 1100$): At the extremes ($|z| \ge 2.5$), player density drops below $1\%$. Here, unearned historical inertia allows inactive veterans to "camp" at top ranks for years unless an active variance expansion releases ratings back into competition.

To solve this, GlickoD* establishes Gaussian Tail Scaling with Golden Ratio ($\phi$) Powers:

$$ z = \frac{\mu - \mu_0}{\sigma_{\text{pop}}} = \frac{\mu - 1500.0}{175.0} $$ $$ \tau(z) = 1.0 - \exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2\phi}\right) \in [0.0, 1.0] $$ $$ \alpha(z) = 1.0 + (\phi - 1) \cdot \tau(z) \cdot 0.5 $$ $$ \lambda(z) = 1.0 + \phi^{-1} \cdot \tau(z) \cdot 0.5 $$

  • In the Elite Tails ($|z| \ge 2.5 \implies \mu \ge 1950$): $\tau(z) \to 1.0 \implies \alpha$ expands by $(\phi - 1)/2 \approx +30.9\%$ while regression $\lambda$ increases by $\phi^{-1}/2 \approx +30.9\%$. 📐 Bayesian DAG Invariance: Why Opponent Updates Require a Frozen Prior

A frequent question is: "Why do opponents calculate rating changes against a stabilized prior rather than a fluctuating provisional rating?"

In a Bayesian rating network, competitive history forms a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) across time. If a newcomer's rating dynamically fluctuates during matches 1 through 15 while opponents also adjust their ratings simultaneously, it triggers recursive feedback loops:

  • Opponent A's rating update would depend on the newcomer's provisional rating after Game 3.
  • The newcomer's provisional rating after Game 3 would depend on Opponent B from Game 2.
  • Opponent B's rating would later shift after playing Opponent C, which retroactively invalidates Opponent A's update.

By discovering the newcomer's true emergent prior $R_{15}$ in Pass 1 and calculating opponents' outcomes against that stabilized baseline in Pass 2, the initial 15 games function as a pure, objective measurement probe. Opponents face an accurate skill estimate without cyclical feedback, preserving mathematical tractability and tournament integrity.
