Frequently Asked Questions & Model Guide

In-depth scientific documentation of the rating algorithms, multiplayer adjustments, and leaderboard mechanics.

What are the three rating models, and how do they differ?

The portal computes three distinct rating models on the exact same competitive dataset of over 131,000 matches:

  • Glicko-2 Standard: The classical online leaderboard benchmark created by Mark Glickman. Think of Glicko-2 as an odometer moving forward trip-by-trip: each match immediately updates your rating, but past results are locked in stone forever. In multiplayer games, it treats every match as separate independent 1v1 duels at full weight ($w = 1.0$).
  • Glicko-2 MP-Weighted (Coulom/Glickman Fractional Weighting): Variance-calibrated specifically for 3-player and 4-player games. When an $N$-player match is decomposed into $N-1$ pairwise subgames, scaling the match weight ($w = \frac{1}{N-1}$) ensures that the match contributes exactly one match's worth of statistical evidence to both the rating shift ($\Delta \mu$) and the rating deviation ($\mathrm{RD}$) shrinkage, preventing artificial overconfidence and runaway rating inflation in multiplayer formats.
  • Whole-History Rating (WHR): Created by French AI researcher Rémi Coulom (2008). Rather than an odometer advancing day by day, think of WHR as a retrospective biographer reviewing an entire career from beginning to end. Using a Bayesian state-space model and Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation, WHR models skill changing smoothly over time via continuous Brownian motion. It uses future games backwards in time to clean up early noise and reveal a player's true career trajectory.

Whole-History Rating (WHR): How Does It Work and How Does It Differ From Glicko?

Whole-History Rating (WHR) represents a major philosophical evolution in competitive rating design. Traditional systems like Elo and Glicko evaluate games one-by-one in strict chronological sequence. Once a rating is stamped on day $t$, it is permanent—even if your opponent was completely unrated at the time and later went on to win the World Championship. WHR solves for every player's skill on every day simultaneously across the entire 10-year timeline.

1. The Core Model: Skill Drift Over Time (Brownian Motion)

In real life, players don't wake up on day 100 with the exact same sharpness they had on day 1—they improve, change strategies, or get a bit rusty during breaks. WHR reflects this physical reality by modeling skill as a continuous one-dimensional Brownian motion (Wiener process). The uncertainty in skill drift between day $t$ and day $t + \Delta t$ expands in direct proportion to elapsed calendar days:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(r_{t + \Delta t} - r_t) = w^2 \cdot \Delta t $$

Here, $w^2$ is the daily process variance ($w^2 = 0.0016$ for competitive TTA). If you take a 100-day hiatus, the system does not arbitrarily demote you—it simply widens its confidence margin ($100 \times w^2$), ready to snap back into sharp focus as soon as you sit down at the board again.

2. The Career Biographer: Global Optimization (MAP Estimation)

Instead of updating a single number match-by-match, WHR pools all 131,000+ matches and 3,489 competitors across 2017–2026 into a joint posterior probability distribution and finds the most probable skill history for every player:

$$ P(\mathbf{r} \mid \mathbf{D}) \propto P(\mathbf{r}) \prod_{k=1}^{M} P(\text{Outcome}_k \mid \mathbf{r}) $$

WHR solves this massive system using multidimensional Newton-Raphson optimization accelerated by the linear-time Thomas block-tridiagonal algorithm, operating as a mathematically optimal Kalman smoother.

In plain terms: If you beat an unrated player in 2021 who subsequently won 20 consecutive master tournaments, WHR recognizes in retrospect that your 2021 victory was achieved against someone genuinely world-class, rather than a fluke against a beginner.

4. Custom Engine Innovation: The Golden Retrospective ($1/\phi$) & A Concrete 3-Year Example

Textbook WHR (Coulom 2008) operates without horizon boundaries. While mathematically elegant, unbounded retrospective smoothers introduce a serious flaw: The Time Machine Problem.

A Concrete 3-Year Scenario: Alice vs. Bob

Suppose that in 2023, Alice plays a league match and defeats Bob. At that time, Bob is an inexperienced novice who makes elementary tactical blunders. Over the next three years (2024–2026), Bob studies high-level strategy, logs 500 competitive matches, and ascends to become a 2100-rated World Champion.

Under textbook unbounded WHR, Bob's 2026 mastery leaks completely backward through time into 2023. The system retroactively treats Alice's 2023 victory as if she defeated a peak 2100 grandmaster! Alice receives an undeserved, massive rating boost for defeating someone who was actually a beginner on that day.

How our Golden Retrospective Engine fixes this: We apply an exact exponential attenuation kernel based on the Golden Ratio ($\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.61803$):

$$ W(\Delta t) = \phi^{-2 \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{365.25}} $$

Because three full years elapsed between Alice's match and Bob's championship peak ($\Delta t \approx 1095$ days), the retrospective weight drops to $\phi^{-6} = (1/\phi)^6 \approx 0.090$ (only 9.0% information leakage). Modern results cannot rewrite historical tournament eras. Alice is rated on Bob's genuine skill near the time they actually played, preserving historical reality while still smoothing out match-to-match noise.

  • Annual Information Retention ($1/\phi \approx 0.618$): Due to the golden ratio identity $1 - \frac{1}{\phi} = \frac{1}{\phi^2}$, each elapsed year retains exactly $\frac{1}{\phi} \approx 61.80\%$ of future calibration volume:
    • Year 1: Retains $\frac{1}{\phi} \approx 61.80\%$ retrospective calibration volume.
    • Year 2: Retains $\frac{1}{\phi^3} \approx 23.61\%$ retrospective calibration volume.
    • Year 3: Retains $\frac{1}{\phi^5} \approx 9.02\%$ retrospective calibration volume.
    • Year 4+: Long-tail leakage drops below $2.1\%$, completely preventing distant modern results from distorting historical eras.
    • Mathematical Conservation: $\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\phi^{2k-1}} = 1.00000$ (100% total information conservation without ad-hoc cutoffs).
  • Active Leaderboard $t_{\mathrm{now}}$ Forward Projection: On the active leaderboard, ratings are evaluated at the present day $t_{\mathrm{now}} = \max(t_{\mathrm{database}})$. When a player stops competing, daily Brownian motion continuously expands their uncertainty:
    $$ \sigma^2(t_{\mathrm{now}}) = \sigma^2(t_{\mathrm{last}}) + w^2 \cdot (t_{\mathrm{now}} - t_{\mathrm{last}}) $$
    Because the Conservative Rating subtracts $3 \times \mathrm{RD}(t_{\mathrm{now}})$, long-retired players gently step aside on the active ladder, while their peak historical ratings remain permanently honored in their career timeline.

Summary: While Glicko-2 is ideal for live matchmaking queues requiring instant, one-pass updates, our Golden Retrospective WHR is the gold standard for historical analysis, blending peak skill detection with era integrity and automated leaderboard defense.

5. Retrospective Smoothing vs. True Forward Prediction ($t+1$ Filter-Smoother Duality)

A frequent question from competitive players is: "If WHR achieves lower calibration error on historical match logs, does that mean it also predicts tomorrow's games better than Glicko-2?"

In signal processing and time-series statistics, the answer is an emphatic no. This is the classic distinction between an online filter and a fixed-interval smoother:

  • Glicko-2 is an Online Filter: It only ever knows about games in the past ($\le t$). It updates forward in time with built-in Bayesian shrinkage ($g(\mathrm{RD})$), specifically designed to prevent overreacting to short-term variance.
  • WHR is a Retrospective Smoother: It calculates skill trajectories by finding a joint compromise across all games before and after $t$. When evaluated retrospectively on historical records, it benefits from having already seen future games.
  • The "Boundary Effect" at $t_{\mathrm{now}}$: At the present moment, WHR has no future matches to anchor its trajectory. If a player goes on a 4-game hot streak, WHR's unconstrained Newton update spikes their endpoint rating sharply upward. In our 53-month walk-forward predictive benchmark (2022–2026), this creates forward overconfidence:
    • Glicko-2 MP-Weighted achieves an out-of-sample Brier score of 0.2140 and Expected Calibration Error of just 1.16%.
    • WHR trails with an out-of-sample Brier score of 0.2185 and an Expected Calibration Error of 3.48% (3× higher error due to endpoint volatility).

Conclusion: Use WHR for historical biography and peak career evaluation; use Glicko-2 for live matchmaking, ladder ranking, and predicting tomorrow's tournament matchups.

Which model is the most accurate?

For retrospective analysis of historic performance and career peak detection, Whole-History Rating (WHR) offers the highest predictive accuracy and smoothest rating trajectories because it uses complete information across all seasons.

For live, online leaderboard tracking that directly corrects multiplayer inflation while maintaining forward-only updates, Glicko-2 MP-Weighted is mathematically optimal.

What is the Conservative Rating (C-Rating)?

The leaderboard ranks players by their Conservative Rating ($C$) rather than raw rating ($\mu$). It represents the lower bound of a 99.7% confidence interval:

$$C = \mu - 3 \times \sigma \quad \text{or in Glicko scale:} \quad C = \text{Rating} - 3 \times \text{RD}$$

For example, a player with Rating 1800 and RD 50 has $C = 1800 - 150 = 1650.00$. A new player with Rating 1800 but RD 350 has $C = 1800 - 1050 = 750.00$.

Why rank by C-Rating instead of raw Rating?

Ranking by raw rating rewards "volatility camping"—a player could join, win 2 fortunate games, reach 1750, and sit at the top of the leaderboard without facing tough opponents.

By penalizing uncertainty ($3 \times \text{RD}$), C-Rating requires players to play consistently to narrow their RD before they can earn high ranks. As you play more games, your RD decreases and your C-Rating climbs towards your actual skill rating.

What is the "multiplayer fractional weighting" problem?

In Through the Ages, matches are played with 2, 3, or 4 players. When a 4-player game is decomposed into pairwise encounters under standard Bradley-Terry assumptions, it generates $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairwise comparisons.

In naive Glicko-2, each player is treated as if they fought 3 completely independent, full-length 1v1 duels. This accumulates 3 times more statistical evidence than physically occurred in a single board game! As a result, Rating Deviation ($\mathrm{RD}$) collapses unnaturally fast, making the engine artificially overconfident in players who primarily play 4-player games.

$$\text{Coulom Fractional Weight:} \quad w = \frac{1}{N - 1}$$ $$\text{For 2-player duel:} \quad w = \frac{1}{2 - 1} = 1.00$$ $$\text{For 3-player game:} \quad w = \frac{1}{3 - 1} = 0.50$$ $$\text{For 4-player game:} \quad w = \frac{1}{4 - 1} = 0.333$$

By weighting each encounter by $w = \frac{1}{N-1}$ as formulated by Rémi Coulom, the weight scales both the rating adjustment ($\Delta \mu$) and the uncertainty shrinkage ($1/v$). This ensures that an $N$-player game provides the statistical weight of exactly one match rather than $N-1$ separate matches, preventing both runaway rating inflation and unnatural $\mathrm{RD}$ collapse in multiplayer tournaments.

Why Introduce Season Resets? Career Inertia vs. Seasonal Stakes

In traditional continuous rating systems, a player who has competed in over 1,000 matches accumulates a very narrow Rating Deviation ($\mathrm{RD} \approx 30$). While this provides exceptional historical precision, it can introduce career legacy inertia: recent performance during a stellar calendar year only shifts their rating by a few points, while newer active contenders struggle to displace inactive veterans who established high ratings years ago.

To preserve historical depth while rewarding active seasonal form, the portal introduces two optional, mathematically unified Golden Ratio ($\phi \approx 1.618034$) season reset mechanisms:

  • Continuous (Standard): Uninterrupted lifetime ratings (the traditional baseline).
  • Soft Season Reset ($\phi^1$ Inflation, $\phi^{-3}$ Tier Reversion): Gentle annual refresh preserving career hierarchy while giving active seasonal form greater weight.
  • Hard Season Reset (“Amplified”, $\phi^2$ Inflation, $\phi^{-1}$ Tier Reversion): Dynamic seasonal reset allowing the top active tournament performers of each season to surge to the summit of the leaderboard.

Mathematical Formulations: Pure Golden Ratio ($\phi$) Harmonization

Both reset modes operate at every calendar year boundary (e.g. crossing from 2024 to 2025, and 2025 to 2026), applying mathematical transformations derived strictly from powers of the Golden Ratio ($\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.61803398875$):

1. Soft Season Reset Formula

Uncertainty is expanded by a factor of $\phi$, while ratings gently shrink toward the mean of their International Championship competitive tier by $\lambda_{\text{soft}} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\phi}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{\phi} = \frac{1}{\phi^3} \approx 0.236068$ (approximately $23.61\%$):

$$ \mathrm{RD}_{\text{new}} = \min(350.0, \;\mathrm{RD} \cdot \phi^1) $$
$$ \mu_{\text{new}} = (1 - \phi^{-3})\,\mu + \phi^{-3}\,\mu_{\text{tier}} \approx 0.7639\,\mu + 0.2361\,\mu_{\text{tier}} $$

2. Hard Season Reset (“Amplified”) Formula

Uncertainty is expanded by $\phi^2 \approx 2.618034$, while ratings strongly revert toward their competitive tier mean by $\lambda_{\text{hard}} = \frac{1}{\phi} = \phi^{-1} \approx 0.618034$ ($61.80\%$):

$$ \mathrm{RD}_{\text{new}} = \min(350.0, \;\mathrm{RD} \cdot \phi^2) $$
$$ \mu_{\text{new}} = (1 - \phi^{-1})\,\mu + \phi^{-1}\,\mu_{\text{tier}} \approx 0.3820\,\mu + 0.6180\,\mu_{\text{tier}} $$

Tournament Rating Eligibility Policy (“Glicko-Eligible”)

Not all competitive events in the TTA ecosystem share the same structural format. Some community tournaments (such as fast-paced Swiss Quick & Dirty cups or exhibition events) have unorthodox tiebreakers or differing turn clocks that the community prefers not to factor into official Elo/Glicko ratings.

To support complete transparency without polluting rating integrity:

  • Every completed tournament match is preserved in the comprehensive match ledger (matches and pairwise_matches).
  • Tournaments marked as non-Glicko eligible remain fully visible on player match histories and are included in the rolling Community Leaderboard sanity checks.
  • The Glicko-2 and Whole-History Rating calculators strictly filter on WHERE glicko_eligible = 1, ensuring exhibition matches never distort official competitive skill ratings.

Webmaster “Last Update” Deltas

When inspecting leaderboard deltas, selecting Last Update evaluates rating movement relative to the exact snapshot preceding the latest batch of tournament matches committed by the Webmaster. This provides an immediate, distortion-free view of how the most recent tournament results altered the global standings.
